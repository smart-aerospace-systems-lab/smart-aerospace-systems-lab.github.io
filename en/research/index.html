Research

Our research covers flapping-wing aerial vehicles, vision-based UAM surveillance systems, and UAM electric propulsion.

Flapping-Wing Aerial Vehicle

We study the actuation mechanisms and wing structures of bird-inspired flapping-wing aerial vehicles. We evaluate the durability of the drive system through gear-train design and multibody dynamics analysis, and experimentally analyze how the flexibility and dynamic characteristics of the wing structure affect thrust and lift generation.

We design and build tailed flapping-wing aerial vehicles with a wingspan of about 1.5 m. On a ground test rig we measure thrust, lift and power consumption to analyze how the wing's structural dynamics relate to propulsive performance, and we measure the flapping state in flight for gliding flight and flight control. Because they are hard to tell apart from real birds, these vehicles can be used for surveillance and reconnaissance and for bird control at airports and orchards.

  • Structural Dynamics
  • Kinematic Design
  • Flight Control System

Related projectDevelopment of Structural Technologies for Biomimetic Unmanned Vehicles

Hall Sensor Integration and Flight Performance Evaluation for Gliding Flight

We calibrate Hall-effect and current sensors to measure wing position and motor load together, and use ensemble averaging and FFT to reduce noise and compare loads between the upstroke and downstroke. We then check whether gliding is possible for different wing structural dynamics.

Thrust Performance and Wing Structural Dynamics

We identify the natural frequencies of three wings with different stiffness using impact tests and FFT, and estimate torque from motor current and rotational speed to compare thrust and energy efficiency. In the low-power range, flapping was more energy-efficient than a propeller.

Modal Analysis of Lightweight Structures Using Motion Capture Cameras

To avoid the mass-loading effect of accelerometers, we perform non-contact modal analysis with motion capture cameras. The first mode agreed well with Ansys finite element analysis, and we also applied the method to a fabric-covered wing in the 5–8 Hz flapping range.

Vision-based Surveillance System

We develop systems that measure and monitor the position of UAM aircraft taking off from and landing at vertiports. We build a UWB–LiDAR fusion auxiliary positioning system and a stereo-camera-based final approach path monitoring system, and analyze how structural vibration caused by rotor downwash affects measurement accuracy.

In dense urban areas with many high-rise buildings, GNSS and radio-based navigation aids alone may not be enough to monitor take-offs and landings. We develop a stereo vision system that computes an aircraft's 3D position from two camera images, together with a UWB–LiDAR fusion auxiliary positioning system, and validate their performance with ROS 2/Gazebo simulations and motion-capture reference data.

  • Ground Guidance System
  • System Integration
  • Computer Vision

Related projectDevelopment of Design and Construction Technologies for Portable Modular Vertiports and a Surveillance System

Stereo-Camera Depth Estimation on the Vertiport Approach Path

We build a stereo model that accounts for the intrinsic parameters and relative pose of the left and right cameras, and triangulate the target center found by CNN-based object detection to estimate 3D position and range. We simulate vertiport approach scenarios in ROS 2 and Gazebo to validate performance.

Effect of Downwash-Induced Vertiport Structural Vibration

Using finite element analysis, we analyze the displacement and attitude angles that eVTOL rotor excitation frequencies induce in a modular vertiport, and find the camera mounting location with the least vibration to keep position measurements reliable.

UWB–LiDAR Fusion Auxiliary Positioning

We improve accuracy by fusing noise-robust UWB positioning with LiDAR point clouds clustered by K-Means, and study how to integrate the system with PX4 flight control.

UAM Electric Propulsion

We study test and evaluation and fault diagnosis of distributed electric propulsion systems for multicopters and eVTOL aircraft. We test how rotor blade damage affects propulsive performance and develop damage diagnosis methods based on acoustic signals. We are also building a test environment that uses an iron bird and hybrid HILS to reproduce hazardous in-flight situations on the ground.

We build a hybrid HILS (iron-bird type) test environment that combines real propulsion hardware with virtual propulsion models, and inject faults such as propeller breakage and sensor anomalies to collect thrust, torque, current, voltage and rotational speed data. These data form the training basis for avionics software models that detect and reason about abnormal situations.

  • Electric Propulsion System Monitoring
  • Rotor Aerodynamics
  • Structural Interaction

Related projectDevelopment and Demonstration of an Onboard Intelligent Avionics Software Platform for Recognizing and Responding to Abnormal Situations in Urban Air Mobility

Changes in Electric Propulsion Characteristics Due to Rotor Blade Damage

Using a test rig that simulates blade damage, we quantify how thrust, torque and current signal patterns change with each damage type, identifying features for anomaly detection algorithms and building a baseline dataset.

Fault Injection Testing with Hybrid HILS

We design a test environment that injects faults into real propulsion hardware while computing vehicle dynamics and the propulsion system in real-time simulation, and compare UAM/eVTOL ground test cases.

eVTOL Propulsion Ground Testing and Acoustic Diagnosis

Using the iron bird, we test partial ground effect on rooftop vertiports and interference between rotors and their support structures, and verify whether propeller damage can be diagnosed from acoustic signals recorded by low-cost microphones and AR glasses.
